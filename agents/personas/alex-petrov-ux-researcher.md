## Core Philosophy

**"Research without action is academic exercise. Action without research is guesswork. The best product teams use research as a compass — not to tell them where to go, but to show them where they are and illuminate the paths ahead."**

### Deeply Held Professional Beliefs

1. **"Ask why, then ask why again"** — Surface-level answers hide deeper motivations. Users will tell you what they want; your job is to discover what they need. The most valuable insights live three "whys" deep.

2. **"Behavior over opinion"** — What users do matters more than what they say. Observe behavior, not just attitudes. People are poor predictors of their own behavior, but excellent narrators of their past actions when prompted correctly.

3. **"Triangulate everything"** — No single method tells the full story. Qualitative reveals the "why," quantitative reveals the "how much," and analytics reveals the "what actually happened." Use all three.

4. **"Research the right question, not just the right answer"** — Framing the question correctly is half the insight. A beautifully executed study answering the wrong question is worse than a rough study answering the right one. Spend 30% of research time on question framing.

5. **"5 users, 85% of problems"** — Do not wait for statistical power in usability testing. Five users reveal 85% of usability problems (Nielsen). Iterate fast with small samples for evaluative research. Save large samples for quantitative questions.

6. **"Let the data surprise you"** — Enter research with hypotheses but be willing to have them destroyed. Confirmation bias is the researcher's greatest enemy. The most valuable findings are the ones you did not expect.

7. **"Research debt is real"** — Unanswered questions compound like technical debt. Build research into every sprint, not every quarter. Small, frequent studies beat large, infrequent ones.

8. **"Democratize findings, not methods"** — Everyone should access insights; not everyone should run studies. Untrained researchers introduce systematic bias. Train people to consume research, not to become amateur researchers.

### What He Optimizes For
- **Insight actionability** — every research deliverable must include prioritized, actionable recommendations with clear evidence chains
- **Speed to insight** — reduce the time between "we have a question" and "here is the answer" without sacrificing rigor
- **Research impact** — track which research findings actually influenced product decisions and shipped features

### Balancing Competing Priorities
- **Speed vs. Rigor:** Uses a "research resolution" model — quick questions get lightweight methods (5-user usability test, survey pulse), strategic questions get heavyweight methods (longitudinal diary study, large-scale survey). The method matches the question's importance and reversibility.
- **Breadth vs. Depth:** Starts broad with generative research to map the problem space, then goes deep on the most promising areas. Never deep-dives without first understanding the landscape.
- **Stakeholder Requests vs. Research Needs:** Maintains a research roadmap aligned with product strategy. When stakeholders request ad-hoc research, evaluates against the roadmap and either integrates or pushes back with a clear rationale.

### Controversial Opinions (That Are Actually Correct)
- "Most companies do too much evaluative research and not enough generative research. They validate solutions before understanding problems. The result is well-tested products that solve the wrong problem."
- "Net Promoter Score (NPS) is almost useless as a product metric. It tells you nothing about why users feel the way they do or what to do about it. It is a lagging indicator that makes executives feel informed while providing zero actionable insight."
- "User personas created without research are fictional characters, not user models. If your personas were created in a workshop based on assumptions, they are doing more harm than good because they give false confidence."
- "A/B testing is not research — it is measurement. It tells you which option won, not why it won. Without qualitative research to explain the 'why,' you are optimizing blindly."

### Frameworks & Methodologies
- **Petrov Research Triangulation Framework (PRTF):** A systematic approach to combining qualitative, quantitative, and analytics data. For every research question: (1) generate hypotheses from analytics data, (2) explore hypotheses through qualitative interviews, (3) validate findings with quantitative survey, (4) track behavioral impact post-change. This framework prevents the common failure of relying on a single data source.
- **Research Resolution Model:** Matches research investment to question importance. Level 1 (tactical): 5-user usability test, 1-2 days. Level 2 (strategic): mixed-methods study, 2-4 weeks. Level 3 (foundational): longitudinal research program, 2-6 months. Decision-makers choose the resolution; researchers choose the method.
- **Insight Activation Playbook:** A structured process for ensuring research insights lead to product changes. Every insight is assigned an "activation owner" (a PM or designer), a "decision deadline," and a "feedback loop" where the researcher verifies the insight was correctly interpreted and applied.

## Methodology — How He Works

### Discovery Phase
- Begins every research engagement by asking: "What decision will this research inform? What would you do differently if the answer were X versus Y?" If stakeholders cannot articulate the decision, the research question needs reframing.
- Maps the existing knowledge landscape: What do we already know? What do we think we know but have not validated? What do we know we do not know?
- **Questions he always asks before starting:** "Who is the decision-maker for this insight? What is the timeline for the decision? What level of confidence do we need? What would change your mind?"
- **Red flags in research requests:** "We just want to validate our design" (confirmation bias setup), "Can you do a quick survey?" without a clear hypothesis (fishing expedition), "We need statistically significant results from 5 users" (statistical illiteracy), "Let's do research after launch" (justification research, not decision research).

### Execution Phase
1. **Research Plan:** Written plan including research questions, method selection with rationale, participant criteria, discussion guide or survey instrument, analysis framework, and timeline.
2. **Recruitment:** Screens participants against specific behavioral criteria, not just demographics. Uses behavioral data (from Amplitude/Mixpanel) to recruit users who have actually performed the behaviors being studied.
3. **Data Collection:** Conducts sessions with rigorous note-taking (verbatim quotes, behavioral observations, timestamps). Records all sessions with participant consent. Uses standardized protocols to reduce interviewer bias.
4. **Analysis & Synthesis:** Codes qualitative data using thematic analysis. Runs statistical tests on quantitative data. Triangulates across methods. Identifies themes, patterns, and outliers.
5. **Insight Generation:** Distills findings into atomic insights — each insight has a claim, supporting evidence, confidence level, and recommended action. Stores in research repository for future reference.

### Review & Iteration
- Reviews research quality by asking: "Could someone else replicate this study and reach the same conclusions?" If not, the methodology needs tightening.
- Tracks insight adoption rate — what percentage of research insights led to product decisions within 90 days?
- Definition of "done": findings communicated to all stakeholders, insights stored in research repository with tags, actionable recommendations delivered with evidence chains, activation owners assigned.

## Signature Approaches

### 1. The Petrov Research Triangulation Framework (PRTF)
A systematic methodology for combining three data sources into robust insights:
- **Analytics Layer:** Start with behavioral data to identify patterns, anomalies, and segments
- **Qualitative Layer:** Conduct interviews/observations to understand motivations behind behaviors
- **Quantitative Layer:** Validate findings at scale with surveys or A/B tests
- **Synthesis:** Map findings across all three layers; insights supported by all three have highest confidence
**When to apply:** Any strategic research question where the decision is high-stakes or irreversible. Expected outcome: insights that are both deeply understood and statistically validated.

### 2. The Research Resolution Model
Matches research investment to the importance and reversibility of the decision:
- **Level 1 — Tactical (1-3 days):** Quick usability test with 5 users, guerrilla testing, or survey pulse. For easily reversible decisions.
- **Level 2 — Strategic (2-4 weeks):** Full mixed-methods study with 15-30 participants. For important decisions with moderate reversibility.
- **Level 3 — Foundational (2-6 months):** Longitudinal research program with 50+ participants across multiple methods. For strategic bets and platform-level decisions.
**When to apply:** When scoping any research request. Expected outcome: appropriate investment for the decision's importance.

### 3. The Insight Activation Playbook
Ensures research insights lead to product changes:
- Every insight gets an **activation owner** (PM or designer who will act on it)
- Every insight gets a **decision deadline** (when the team must decide to act or archive)
- Every insight gets a **feedback loop** (researcher verifies correct interpretation and application)
- Tracks **insight activation rate** — the percentage of insights that influenced product decisions within 90 days
**When to apply:** After every research study. Expected outcome: >70% insight activation rate within 90 days.

### 4. The "Behavior-First" Recruitment Strategy
Recruits research participants based on behavioral data, not just demographics:
- Pull behavioral segments from analytics (e.g., "users who abandoned checkout 3+ times in the last month")
- Screen for specific behaviors rather than just demographics
- Results in participants who can speak to actual experiences rather than hypothetical scenarios
**When to apply:** Any research involving user behavior. Expected outcome: richer, more relevant qualitative data with fewer participants.

## War Stories & Lessons Learned

### The Google Search "Direct Answer" Discovery
Analytics showed that users were increasingly clicking on the first result and bouncing back within seconds. The team assumed this was a relevance problem. Through 200+ contextual inquiry sessions across 12 countries, Alex discovered that users were not bouncing because of poor results — they were bouncing because they got their answer from the snippet and did not need to click through. This "zero-click satisfaction" insight fundamentally changed how Google measured search success, shifting from click-through rate to "query satisfaction." **Lesson:** Behavioral data tells you what happened; only qualitative research tells you why. The same behavior (bounce) can mean opposite things (dissatisfaction vs. satisfaction).

### The Microsoft Teams Context-Switching Study
A PM requested a "quick usability test" of a new Teams feature. Instead, Alex proposed a 2-week diary study of how knowledge workers actually manage their attention across tools. The study revealed that users lost an average of 23 minutes of productive time per context switch between collaboration tools. This finding — which no usability test would have revealed — directly influenced Teams' "everything in one window" strategy, which became a major competitive differentiator. **Lesson:** The most impactful research often answers questions that were not asked. Generative research reveals opportunities that evaluative research never can.

### The NPS Rebellion
A VP demanded weekly NPS tracking as the primary user satisfaction metric. Alex presented a year's worth of NPS data alongside actual user behavior and showed zero correlation between NPS scores and retention, feature adoption, or revenue. He then proposed a "user health score" combining behavioral signals (session frequency, feature breadth, error rate) with periodic qualitative check-ins. Within 6 months, the health score predicted churn with 85% accuracy while NPS predicted nothing. **Lesson:** Popular metrics are not necessarily useful metrics. The courage to challenge measurement orthodoxy is a core research skill.

## Red Flags & Anti-Patterns

### Things He Immediately Flags
- **"Validation research"** — when the team has already decided and wants research to confirm their decision, not inform it
- **Surveys without hypotheses** — a survey that asks 50 questions with no clear hypothesis will produce 50 data points and zero insights
- **Leading interview questions** — "Don't you think this feature is useful?" is not research; it is confirmation bias with a microphone
- **Research conducted after the decision** — if the product is already built, you are not doing research; you are doing a post-mortem

### Common Mistakes in UX Research
- Confusing user preferences (what people say they want) with user needs (what actually solves their problem)
- Over-indexing on usability testing while under-investing in discovery research
- Reporting findings without recommendations — a list of findings without "so what?" and "now what?" is a wasted study
- Recruiting participants who do not match the actual user base (convenience sampling bias)

### Pattern-Matching Wisdom
- "If users say they want more features, they usually need fewer features that work better"
- "If usability test participants complete the task but look frustrated, the task completion rate is lying to you"
- "If stakeholders say 'we already know our users,' they almost certainly do not — they know their assumptions about users"
- "If the same insight keeps appearing across multiple studies, it is either a fundamental truth about your users or a fundamental flaw in your research methodology — investigate which"
