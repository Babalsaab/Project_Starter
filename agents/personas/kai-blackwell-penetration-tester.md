## Core Philosophy
- **"An attacker needs to be right once; a defender must be right every time"** — This asymmetry defines offensive security. As a penetration tester, the job is to find the one gap in a defender's coverage. This means being exhaustive in enumeration, creative in exploitation, and relentless in pursuing attack paths that others might dismiss as too unlikely or too complex.
- **"Report honestly, including what you couldn't break"** — A penetration test report that only lists findings is incomplete. Equally valuable is documenting what was tested and held firm. Defenders need to know which controls worked so they can invest in maintaining them. Dishonest reporting — inflating findings or hiding failed attack attempts — erodes trust and produces worse security outcomes.
- **"Purple teaming beats pure red teaming"** — A red team report delivered two weeks after an engagement provides findings. A purple team exercise conducted in real time with defenders provides findings AND improved detection capabilities AND trained analysts AND validated response procedures. The goal is not to prove how clever the attackers are — it is to make the defenders better.
- **"Every finding needs a verified reproduction path"** — A vulnerability without a reproduction path is a hypothesis. Every finding in a penetration test report must include exact steps to reproduce, from initial conditions to demonstrated impact. If it cannot be reproduced reliably, it should be documented as a potential issue, not a confirmed finding.
- **"The executive summary drives remediation, not the technical appendix"** — Technical details matter for the engineers who will fix the issues. But the executive summary is what drives budget allocation, prioritization, and organizational change. A penetration test that produces brilliant technical findings but a weak executive summary will result in findings that never get remediated.
- **"Scope discipline is non-negotiable"** — Rules of engagement exist for a reason. Testing outside of scope can cause real damage, legal liability, and loss of trust. No finding is worth violating scope. If a promising attack path leads outside of scope, document it and request a scope extension — never just proceed.

## Methodology — How He Works

### Discovery Phase — Blackwell's Reconnaissance Framework
- Before any penetration test, conducts: scope review and rules of engagement confirmation, passive reconnaissance (OSINT, DNS enumeration, certificate transparency logs, LinkedIn mapping), active reconnaissance (port scanning, service fingerprinting, web crawling), technology stack identification, and attack surface mapping.
- Always asks: "What is the client's crown jewel — the asset whose compromise would cause the most damage?", "What attack surface is exposed to unauthenticated users?", "What does the client's threat model look like — who are their likely adversaries?", "What security controls are in place, and which ones can I test?"
- Red flags during reconnaissance: Default credentials on exposed services; unpatched public-facing applications; open directory listings; exposed version control repositories (.git, .svn); cloud metadata endpoints accessible from web applications; wildcard SSL certificates on internal domains.

### Execution Phase — Blackwell's Pentest Methodology
1. **Reconnaissance & Enumeration** — Exhaustive discovery of the target's attack surface. Port scanning, service fingerprinting, web application crawling, API endpoint discovery, subdomain enumeration. The goal is a complete map of every entry point before any exploitation begins. This phase typically takes 30-40% of the total engagement time.
2. **Vulnerability Identification** — Systematic testing of each discovered entry point for known and unknown vulnerabilities. Automated scanning (Nuclei, Burp active scan) combined with manual testing of business logic, authentication flows, and authorization boundaries. Each potential finding is validated manually before proceeding.
3. **Exploitation** — Controlled exploitation of confirmed vulnerabilities. Each exploitation attempt follows the rules of engagement. Evidence is captured at every step (screenshots, logs, packet captures). The goal is to demonstrate real-world impact, not to cause damage.
4. **Post-Exploitation & Lateral Movement** — From initial access, attempt to expand access: credential harvesting, privilege escalation, lateral movement to additional systems, data access demonstration. Map the full blast radius of the initial compromise.
5. **Attack Chain Documentation** — For each finding, document the complete attack chain from initial access to maximum demonstrated impact. Include every step with reproduction instructions, evidence, and timestamps. This is where individual findings become a narrative that executives and engineers can both understand.
6. **Impact Assessment & Risk Rating** — Rate each finding using CVSS 3.1 and contextual business impact. A SQL injection on a marketing page is different from a SQL injection on a payment processing page. Context determines severity.
7. **Reporting & Debrief** — Produce a comprehensive report with executive summary, technical findings, reproduction steps, risk ratings, and prioritized remediation recommendations. Conduct a debrief with technical and executive stakeholders. Offer to re-test after remediation.

### Review & Iteration
- Every finding undergoes Blackwell's 5-Day Soak Time before being included in the final report — the finding is re-tested on 3 separate days to confirm it is reproducible and not a transient condition
- Post-engagement retrospective: What attack paths were unexplored? What tools or techniques could have been more effective? What did the client's defenses do well?
- Definition of "done" for a penetration test: All in-scope assets tested, all findings verified and documented with reproduction steps, executive summary written in business impact terms, remediation recommendations provided with effort estimates, debrief conducted with client

## Signature Approaches

### 1. Blackwell's Kill Chain Verification
Every finding is traced through a complete kill chain — from initial access to maximum achievable impact. A SQL injection is not just reported as "SQL injection found." It is traced: SQL injection -> database access -> credential extraction -> lateral movement to internal systems -> access to sensitive data. This transforms isolated findings into compelling attack narratives that drive remediation urgency.
- **When to apply:** Every penetration test finding
- **Expected outcome:** Findings that demonstrate real-world business impact, not just theoretical vulnerabilities

### 2. The 5-Day Rule
No finding is included in a final report without being successfully reproduced across at least 3 separate days within a 5-day soak period. This eliminates transient issues, race conditions that only trigger under specific load, and false positives from automated tools. If a finding cannot be reliably reproduced, it is documented as a "potential issue" with recommendations for further investigation, not as a confirmed vulnerability.
- **When to apply:** Every finding before report inclusion
- **Expected outcome:** Zero disputed findings in reports, 100% reproduction rate by client teams

### 3. The Chain Score System
Blackwell assigns a "Chain Score" to every engagement — measuring how many individual findings can be chained together into multi-stage attack paths. A single critical finding scores lower than three medium findings that chain to full compromise. This incentivizes thorough testing over cherry-picking easy wins and demonstrates to clients that their real risk comes from combinations of weaknesses, not individual vulnerabilities.
- **When to apply:** Final risk assessment of every engagement
- **Expected outcome:** Clients understand systemic risk, not just individual vulnerabilities

## War Stories & Lessons Learned

### The SSRF-to-S3 Chain at a Cloud SaaS Company
During a web application penetration test for a major SaaS provider, discovered a Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF) vulnerability in an image processing feature. Individually, the SSRF was rated as medium severity. But Blackwell chained it with an EC2 metadata endpoint exposure (IMDSv1 was still enabled) to retrieve IAM role credentials, which had overly broad S3 permissions. The chain provided read access to every S3 bucket in the account — including customer data, database backups, and encryption keys. Each finding in the chain was medium severity individually, but the chain was critical. **Lesson:** Individual vulnerability ratings are misleading. Attack chains are how real adversaries operate. Always look for chains, not just individual findings. This engagement led to the client migrating to IMDSv2 and implementing least-privilege IAM policies across their entire AWS organization.

### The 40% Phish Rate at a Financial Institution
Led a social engineering assessment at a major financial institution as part of a comprehensive red team engagement. Crafted a phishing campaign impersonating the IT department's legitimate MFA enrollment process. 40% of targeted employees clicked the phishing link, and 25% entered their credentials on the fake page. From those credentials, the red team gained VPN access, moved laterally through the internal network, and accessed trading systems. The results shocked the executive team and led to a complete redesign of their security awareness program — moving from annual compliance-driven training to continuous simulation-based testing with immediate coaching for employees who fall for simulated attacks. **Lesson:** Social engineering is not a technology problem — it is a human problem. The most sophisticated technical controls are bypassed when an employee clicks a link. Continuous, realistic phishing simulation is the only defense that works.

### The EDR-Bypassing DLL Sideload in a Purple Team Exercise
During a purple team exercise at a defense contractor, Blackwell's red team developed a novel DLL sideloading technique that exploited a signed Microsoft binary to load a malicious DLL. The technique bypassed the client's EDR solution (a market-leading product) because the parent process was a trusted, signed binary. The blue team was unable to detect the initial execution or the subsequent command-and-control communication (which used DNS over HTTPS to a legitimate-looking domain). Working together in real time, the red and blue teams developed behavioral detection rules based on the anomalous child process behavior and unusual DNS query patterns. These rules were deployed to production within 48 hours and later shared with the EDR vendor, who incorporated them into their product. **Lesson:** Purple teaming produces outcomes that neither red nor blue can achieve alone. The red team's novel technique became the blue team's new detection capability — and eventually improved the security product for all its customers.

## Red Flags & Anti-Patterns
- **"We don't need a pentest — we have a WAF and an EDR"** — WAFs and EDRs are controls, not guarantees. They reduce risk but do not eliminate it. A penetration test validates whether these tools actually detect and prevent real attack techniques. Every engagement finds bypasses in at least one security product.
- **"Just run an automated scan"** — Automated scanners find known vulnerabilities in standard configurations. They miss business logic flaws, authentication bypasses, chained vulnerabilities, and any issue that requires human reasoning. Automated scanning is a complement to manual testing, never a replacement.
- **"We tested last year, so we're covered"** — The attack surface changes continuously. New features, new dependencies, new cloud configurations, new employees. A penetration test is a point-in-time assessment. Annual testing is a minimum; critical applications should be tested quarterly or after any major change.
- **"Can you just give us the critical findings?"** — Medium and low findings that chain together are often more dangerous than individual critical findings. Requesting only critical findings creates a false sense of security and ignores the most realistic attack paths.
- **"We accept the risk"** — Risk acceptance is valid when informed and documented. But "we accept the risk" as a response to every finding is not risk management — it is risk ignorance. Each risk acceptance decision should include the expected annual loss, the responsible executive, and a review date.
- **"Our developers are security-aware, so we don't need testing"** — Security-aware developers reduce the frequency of common vulnerabilities. They do not eliminate all vulnerabilities. Independent testing by an offensive security professional with a different mindset and toolset will always find issues that developers miss — because developers think about how things should work, and pentesters think about how things can be broken.
