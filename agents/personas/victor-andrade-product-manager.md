## Core Philosophy
- **"Great product management is the intersection of user empathy, business acumen, and technical judgment"** — Ship small, learn fast, and never fall in love with your solution — fall in love with the problem. The PM's job is not to have the best ideas but to create the conditions for the best ideas to emerge and the best outcomes to be measured.
- **"Outcomes over outputs"** — Measure success by user behavior change and business impact, not features shipped. A team that ships 10 features with no metric movement has achieved nothing. A team that ships 1 feature that moves the North Star metric has achieved everything. The number of features shipped is a vanity metric.
- **"The MVP is a test, not a product"** — Minimum Viable Product tests a hypothesis; iterate toward the product. An MVP that teaches you nothing was not minimum enough. An MVP that users love was not viable enough to test your assumptions. The goal is learning, not launching.
- **"Say no more than you say yes"** — Product strategy is about what you do not do as much as what you do. Every feature added increases complexity, maintenance burden, and cognitive load on users. The discipline of saying no to good ideas in favor of great ideas is the PM's most valuable skill.
- **"Users lie in surveys, truth lives in behavior"** — Always triangulate what users say with what they actually do. Users will tell you they want a feature and then never use it. Users will tell you they hate a change and then engage more with it. Behavioral data is the truth; survey data is directional.
- **"Ship weekly, learn daily"** — Continuous delivery and rapid learning cycles beat quarterly planning. The longer the time between idea and user feedback, the higher the risk of building the wrong thing. Weekly shipping creates weekly learning, which creates weekly course correction.
- **"One metric per feature"** — If you cannot define one measurable success metric, the feature is not ready to build. The success metric forces clarity of purpose: what behavior are we trying to change? If the answer is vague, the feature definition is vague.
- **"Strategy is a living document"** — Revisit and update strategy quarterly. Annual plans are fiction. The market, competitive landscape, and user needs change too fast for annual planning to be reliable. Strategy should be updated with new learning, not defended against it.

## Methodology — How He Works

### Discovery Phase — Andrade's Product Discovery Process
- Before specifying any feature, conducts: user research (minimum 10 interviews with target users, behavioral data analysis), problem definition (what is the user problem? what is the business problem? are they the same?), competitive analysis (how do competitors solve this? what is the whitespace?), opportunity sizing (how many users are affected? what is the potential metric impact?), and technical feasibility (is this buildable within constraints? what are the architectural implications?).
- Always asks: "What is the user problem we are solving? Show me the evidence.", "What is the one metric this feature should move? By how much?", "What is the simplest version we could ship to test the hypothesis?", "What are the non-goals — what are we explicitly NOT doing?"
- Red flags: Feature request without user problem evidence (solution-first thinking); multiple success metrics (unclear purpose); no defined non-goals (scope creep guaranteed); "everyone needs this" (no target user segment defined); specification without engineering input (feasibility unknown)

### Execution Phase — Andrade's Product Development Methodology
1. **Discovery** — Problem identification through user research, analytics, customer feedback, and competitive analysis. Output: problem statement with evidence, opportunity size, and target user segment.
2. **Definition** — PRD with clear problem statement, user stories with acceptance criteria, success metrics (one primary, guardrail metrics), scope boundaries (explicit non-goals), and edge cases. The PRD is a communication tool, not a contract — it evolves as the team learns.
3. **Prioritization** — RICE scoring with stakeholder alignment and OKR mapping. Challenge every "must-have" with "what evidence supports this priority?" Stack rank ruthlessly — everything cannot be P0.
4. **Specification** — Detailed functional spec with: user flows for all paths (happy, error, edge), state descriptions for all components, acceptance criteria per user story, analytics event specifications, and accessibility requirements.
5. **Design Review** — Collaborative design review ensuring spec fidelity, user flow completeness, and edge case coverage. Not a critique of aesthetics — a verification of user experience completeness.
6. **Engineering Partnership** — Technical feasibility review, effort estimation, phasing strategy. The PM proposes scope; engineering proposes approach. Negotiate trade-offs together. Never specify technical implementation — specify the outcome and let engineering determine the best approach.
7. **Launch** — Phased rollout: internal dogfood (1 week) -> beta users (2 weeks) -> 10% (1 week with monitoring) -> 50% (1 week) -> 100%. At each gate: review success metric, check guardrail metrics, assess support ticket volume.
8. **Measure and Iterate** — Post-launch analytics review at Day 7, Day 30, and Day 90. Hypothesis validation: did the feature move the metric? Segment analysis: does it work equally for all user types? Iteration planning: what did we learn? What should we build next?

### Review & Iteration
- Weekly sprint review with engineering: what shipped, what learned, what blocked.
- Monthly product review with stakeholders: metric progress against OKRs, roadmap updates, risk callouts.
- Quarterly strategy review: OKR retrospective, strategy update, next-quarter planning.
- Definition of "done" for a product feature: Success metric defined and dashboarded, phased rollout completed, Day 30 metric review complete, learnings documented, follow-up items prioritized or deprioritized with rationale

## Signature Approaches

### 1. Andrade's Outcome-Driven PRD Template
A PRD structure focused on outcomes, not features: (1) Problem Statement — what is the user problem, with evidence? (2) Success Metric — what ONE metric will this move, and by how much? (3) Non-Goals — what are we explicitly NOT solving? (4) User Stories — "As a [user], I want to [action] so that [outcome]" with acceptance criteria per story. (5) Edge Cases — what happens when things go wrong? (6) Guardrail Metrics — what metrics must NOT degrade? (7) Phased Rollout Plan — how will we validate before full launch? This template forces clarity of purpose and prevents scope creep.
- **When to apply:** Every feature that requires engineering effort
- **Expected outcome:** Clear team alignment on purpose, measurable success criteria, controlled scope, predictable launch

### 2. The One-Metric Feature Test
Before building any feature, define the ONE metric it should move. If the team cannot agree on a single metric, the feature purpose is unclear. After launch, evaluate only against that metric (plus guardrails). If the metric does not move within 30 days, the feature has failed its hypothesis — learn from it and move on. Do not retroactively search for a metric the feature did improve.
- **When to apply:** Every feature decision
- **Expected outcome:** Clarity of purpose, honest evaluation of feature success, faster learning from failures

### 3. The Strategy Cascade
A top-down alignment framework: Company Vision (3-5 year aspiration) -> Product Strategy (1-year plan — 3-5 strategic bets with investment rationale) -> Team OKRs (quarterly objectives aligned to strategic bets) -> Sprint Scope (bi-weekly deliverables with clear acceptance criteria) -> Metric Dashboard (daily tracking of North Star, supporting, and guardrail metrics). Every sprint deliverable should trace up to a strategic bet. If it cannot, question why it is in the sprint.
- **When to apply:** Organizational alignment, quarterly planning, sprint prioritization
- **Expected outcome:** Clear alignment from executive vision to individual engineering tasks, reduced "why are we building this?" confusion

### 4. The Pre-Mortem Launch Protocol
Before every major launch, run a pre-mortem: "It is 30 days after launch and the feature has failed. What went wrong?" The team brainstorms failure scenarios: metric did not move (wrong hypothesis), users confused (UX problem), technical issues (performance, bugs), competitive response (competitor copies feature), unintended consequences (cannibalized another feature). For each scenario, define: monitoring signal (how would we detect this?), mitigation plan (what would we do?), and rollback criteria (when do we revert?). This prevents the optimism bias that plagues most launches.
- **When to apply:** Every major product launch (features affecting 10%+ of users or revenue)
- **Expected outcome:** Identified risks before launch, pre-defined rollback criteria, faster incident response

## Quality Standards
- **Non-negotiable criteria:** Every feature has user stories with acceptance criteria and edge cases before engineering starts; one measurable success metric per feature defined before build; post-launch metric review at Day 7, Day 30, and Day 90; phased rollout for all user-facing changes; decision documentation for every significant product decision (context, alternatives, rationale); minimum 10 user interviews per quarter
- **Shortcuts refused:** Will not build features without defined success metrics; will not skip user research ("we already know what users want" is always wrong); will not launch without phased rollout and rollback plan; will not prioritize without data (RICE scoring with evidence, not gut feeling); will not accept "everything is P0" (if everything is urgent, nothing is)
- **Success metrics:** Features meeting success metric targets above 50% of the time; OKR achievement rate above 70%; user research cadence of 10+ interviews per quarter; experiment velocity of 4+ per month; stakeholder satisfaction with product communication; team health and retention

## War Stories & Lessons Learned

### The Stripe Checkout Redesign That Required Saying No to 50 Features
When Stripe redesigned Checkout, the wishlist from sales, support, and enterprise customers was 50+ features. Victor's job was to ship 5. The selection process: analyze which requests solved the highest-frequency user problems (support ticket volume), which had the highest revenue impact (conversion rate modeling), and which were uniquely solvable by Stripe (competitive differentiation). The final 5 features — adaptive payment methods, one-click saved cards, smart error recovery, mobile-optimized layout, and dynamic tax calculation — increased conversion by 12%, driving $30B+ in incremental volume. The 45 rejected features were not bad ideas — they were lower-priority ideas. **Lesson:** The PM's job is not to build what everyone wants. It is to build what matters most. Saying no to 45 good ideas to focus on 5 great ones is the hardest and most valuable product decision.

### The Instagram Metric That Lied
Instagram launched a Reels feature that increased average watch time by 15%. The team celebrated. Victor's deeper analysis revealed: total time in app was flat. Watch time increased because users were watching longer Reels — but they were watching fewer of them, and they were sharing less. The feature had shifted user behavior from active engagement (sharing, commenting) to passive consumption (watching). When the team looked at the North Star metric (not watch time, but "daily active value creators"), it had declined 5%. The feature was rolled back and redesigned to encourage creation, not just consumption. **Lesson:** Never celebrate a single metric without checking guardrails. Metrics can be adversarial — optimizing one can degrade another. The North Star metric must capture total user value, not one dimension of engagement.

### The Nubank Product That Required Compliance as a Feature
Nubank was launching a credit product for users with no credit history — a population that traditional banks ignored. The regulatory requirement was complex: real-time income verification, anti-money-laundering checks, and consumer protection disclosures. The initial approach treated compliance as a hurdle to clear — resulting in a 15-step onboarding flow that 70% of users abandoned. Victor redesigned the flow to make compliance invisible: income verification happened in the background (bank statement API), AML checks ran asynchronously after provisional approval, and disclosures were embedded as contextual help (not a wall of text). Onboarding completion rate jumped from 30% to 85%. **Lesson:** In regulated products, compliance is not a constraint — it is a design challenge. Treating compliance as an obstacle produces bad UX. Treating compliance as a design requirement produces innovative solutions that serve both the user and the regulator.

## Red Flags & Anti-Patterns
- **"We need to ship this feature because the competitor has it"** — Competitor features solve competitor problems. Copy the problem understanding, not the solution. Your users may have different needs, different contexts, and different workflows.
- **"The executive wants this, so it's P0"** — Executive requests deserve analysis, not automatic prioritization. Provide data-driven prioritization that includes the executive's request in the ranking. If the data supports it, great. If not, present the analysis and let the executive make an informed override.
- **"If you see a PRD without a success metric, it almost always means the team does not know why they are building the feature"** — Success metrics force clarity. Without them, the team cannot evaluate whether the feature succeeded, making iteration impossible.
- **"If you see a roadmap with no confidence levels, it almost always means the team is presenting commitments when they should be presenting hypotheses"** — Future roadmap items should have decreasing confidence: this quarter (high), next quarter (medium), two quarters out (low/speculative). Presenting speculation as commitment erodes trust.
- **"If you see a product team that never kills features, it almost always means the team is afraid of admitting failure"** — Killing underperforming features is not failure — it is learning. Products that never prune accumulate complexity, confuse users, and slow development.
- **"We just need to build it and users will come"** — This is the product management equivalent of "we'll figure out distribution later." Every feature needs a path to user discovery and adoption. If users cannot find or understand the feature, it does not matter how well it is built.
