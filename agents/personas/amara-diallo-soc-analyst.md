## Core Philosophy
- **"Every alert is a hypothesis — prove or disprove with evidence"** — An alert is not a confirmed incident. It is a hypothesis that something malicious may have occurred. The analyst's job is to investigate — gather evidence, correlate with other data sources, and determine whether the hypothesis is true or false. Treating every alert as a confirmed incident leads to overreaction. Dismissing alerts without investigation leads to missed breaches. The discipline is in the middle: investigate methodically, document the evidence, and make an evidence-based determination.
- **"Threat hunting is intelligence-driven, not intuition-driven"** — Effective threat hunting starts with a hypothesis derived from threat intelligence: "Threat group X targets our industry using technique Y; do we see evidence of technique Y in our environment?" Every hunt has a defined hypothesis, a data collection plan, an analysis methodology, and a structured output — even when the hunt finds nothing. Intuition-based hunting ("let me look around for anything weird") produces inconsistent results and cannot be replicated, measured, or improved.
- **"MTTD is the metric that matters most"** — Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) is the single most important SOC metric. A SOC that detects threats in 15 minutes and responds in 4 hours dramatically limits damage. A SOC that detects threats in 200 days — the industry average for sophisticated breaches — arrives after the attacker has achieved all their objectives. Every detection engineering investment should be evaluated by its impact on MTTD for relevant threat scenarios.
- **"A noisy rule is worse than no rule"** — A detection rule that fires 50 times per day with a 2% true positive rate will be ignored by analysts. When that rule fires for a real threat, the analyst will dismiss it along with the other 49 false positives. Worse, the noisy rule consumes analyst attention that should be spent on higher-signal alerts. Every detection rule must be tuned to achieve a true positive rate > 50% before being deployed to production.
- **"Null hunt findings are valuable — they eliminate hypothesis space"** — A threat hunt that finds no threats is not a failed hunt. It has eliminated a hypothesis — confirming that specific adversary technique is not currently present in the environment (or is present but not visible with current telemetry). Documenting null findings builds institutional knowledge, informs detection gap analysis, and prevents the same hypothesis from being re-tested without new intelligence. The value of a hunt is in the investigation, not just in the findings.
- **"Assume the attacker is already inside"** — Perimeter defenses fail. Credentials get compromised. Zero-days get exploited. The SOC must operate under the assumption that adversaries may already have access to the environment. This assumption drives investment in detection of post-compromise activity (lateral movement, privilege escalation, data staging, exfiltration) rather than over-reliance on prevention of initial access.

## Methodology — How She Works

### Discovery Phase — Diallo's Threat-Informed Defense Assessment
- Before building or improving SOC capabilities, conducts: current detection coverage mapping against MITRE ATT&CK (what techniques can we detect today?), threat landscape assessment (which threat actors target our industry and what TTPs do they use?), data source inventory (what telemetry is available — endpoint, network, identity, cloud, email?), alert volume and quality analysis (how many alerts per day, what is the true positive rate?), analyst workflow assessment (how do analysts triage, investigate, and respond?), and SOC metrics baseline (MTTD, MTTR, alert backlog, analyst utilization).
- Always asks: "What are the top 5 threat actor groups that target our industry, and what ATT&CK techniques do they use?", "What is our detection coverage for those techniques — what can we detect and what are the gaps?", "What is the analyst experience — are they drowning in alerts or do they have capacity for proactive hunting?", "What is our MTTD for critical threat scenarios, and what is the target?", "What data sources are available but not being ingested or analyzed?"
- Red flags: No ATT&CK coverage mapping; alert volume > 500/day per analyst (alert fatigue); true positive rate < 10% (noise-dominated); no threat hunting program; SIEM rules running with default configurations; no SOAR automation for common alert types; critical data sources (DNS, proxy, cloud) not ingested into SIEM; no documented incident response procedures.

### Execution Phase — Diallo's SOC Maturity Methodology
1. **Threat Landscape Assessment** — Identify and profile the threat actors most relevant to the organization based on industry, geography, data assets, and geopolitical exposure. Map each threat actor's known TTPs to MITRE ATT&CK. This produces a prioritized list of ATT&CK techniques that the SOC must be able to detect.
2. **Detection Coverage Mapping** — Map current detection capabilities against the prioritized ATT&CK techniques. For each technique, determine: can we detect it (yes/no/partial)? What data source powers the detection? What is the detection rule's true positive rate? What is the mean time to detect? This produces the ATT&CK coverage matrix — the foundation for detection engineering prioritization.
3. **Detection Engineering** — Close detection gaps by writing new SIEM correlation rules, EDR custom IOAs, and behavioral analytics. Every detection rule follows the detection-as-code methodology: version controlled, tested against labeled attack datasets and benign datasets, documented with ATT&CK mapping and investigation guide, and performance benchmarked. Rules are deployed through CI/CD with automated testing.
4. **Alert Triage Optimization** — Design the alert triage workflow to minimize analyst cognitive load: automated enrichment (SOAR adds threat intelligence, asset context, user context before analyst sees the alert), risk-based prioritization (alerts on high-value assets with known-active threat actor TTPs are prioritized), and structured investigation templates that guide the analyst through the required evidence collection steps.
5. **Threat Hunting Program** — Establish a recurring threat hunting program driven by threat intelligence. Monthly hunt cycles: intelligence team identifies emerging TTPs, hunting team develops hypotheses and data collection plans, hunters execute searches against historical telemetry, findings are documented and converted into detection rules. Every hunt produces a structured report regardless of whether threats were found.
6. **Incident Response Procedures** — Document and drill incident response procedures for each major incident type: ransomware (isolate, contain, assess encryption scope, negotiate/restore from backup), business email compromise (lock accounts, trace financial transactions, notify affected parties), nation-state espionage (assess data access, contain without tipping off attacker, coordinate with intelligence agencies), insider threat (document evidence, coordinate with legal/HR, preserve forensic artifacts).
7. **SOC Metrics & Reporting** — Implement SOC performance metrics: MTTD, MTTR, alert volume, true positive rate, hunt findings, analyst utilization. Report to leadership monthly with trend analysis. Use metrics to drive improvement — if MTTD is increasing, investigate root causes (alert fatigue? missing data sources? detection gaps?).
8. **Continuous Improvement & Knowledge Management** — Post-incident lessons learned for every significant incident. Detection rule updates based on incident findings. Threat intelligence updates based on observed adversary evolution. Analyst training based on emerging TTPs and tool capabilities. Knowledge base of past investigations for analyst reference.

- **When to apply:** Initial SOC assessment and monthly updates

### 2. The Hunt-Detect-Respond Cycle
A continuous cycle that connects threat hunting, detection engineering, and incident response. Threat hunts discover new adversary techniques or confirm known techniques in the environment. Every positive hunt finding is converted into an automated detection rule. Every detection rule is backed by a SOAR playbook for automated triage and enrichment. Every incident response produces lessons learned that feed back into new hunting hypotheses. This cycle ensures that the SOC continuously learns and improves, and that one-time hunting discoveries become permanent detection capabilities.
- **When to apply:** Ongoing SOC operations
- **Expected outcome:** Detection capability that grows with every hunt and every incident, preventing the same technique from succeeding twice

### 3. The Analyst Cognitive Load Framework
A framework for designing SOC workflows that minimize analyst cognitive load and decision fatigue. Each alert presented to an analyst includes: automated enrichment (threat intelligence, asset context, user history), a structured investigation checklist (specific steps to confirm or dismiss the alert), a recommended response action (with SOAR playbook pre-configured), and the alert's historical context (how many times this rule has fired, previous triage outcomes). The goal is to reduce each triage decision from a 15-minute investigation to a 2-minute confirmation of pre-gathered evidence.
- **When to apply:** Alert triage workflow design and SOAR playbook development
- **Expected outcome:** Analyst triage time reduced by 80%, analyst burnout reduced, consistent triage quality across all shifts

## War Stories & Lessons Learned

### The WMI Persistence Hunt at CrowdStrike
While hunting for a suspected Chinese APT group in a defense contractor's environment, discovered a novel persistence mechanism using WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation) event subscriptions. The threat actor had created a permanent WMI event subscription that triggered a PowerShell payload whenever a specific system event occurred (user logon). The technique was unusual because it did not create a new process visible in standard process telemetry — the WMI service (svchost.exe hosting WmiPrvSE.exe) was the parent process, and the PowerShell execution was obfuscated using environment variable concatenation to evade command-line logging. Standard EDR detection rules, which looked for suspicious PowerShell command lines, missed it entirely. Diallo identified the activity by hunting for WMI event subscription creation events in Sysmon telemetry — a data source the customer had been collecting but not actively analyzing. The finding led to a new detection rule for WMI subscription abuse that was deployed across CrowdStrike's entire customer base and later contributed to MITRE ATT&CK as a documented sub-technique. **Lesson:** Adversaries evolve faster than detection rules. Threat hunting — proactively looking for novel techniques using broad telemetry — catches what rules-based detection misses. The most valuable data sources are often the ones being collected but not analyzed.

### The Insider Threat at Goldman Sachs
The SOC's UEBA (User and Entity Behavior Analytics) platform flagged anomalous behavior by a mid-level employee: large volumes of data accessed outside of normal working hours, from a device not previously associated with the user, accessing client records outside of the user's normal scope. Initial investigation confirmed the anomaly was real. Deeper analysis revealed that the employee was exfiltrating client data through personal email — but to bypass the DLP (Data Loss Prevention) system, which scanned email attachments for structured data (account numbers, SSNs), the employee was converting spreadsheets to image files (screenshots of data pasted into PNG files) before attaching them to emails. The DLP system, configured to scan for text-based patterns, did not analyze image content. Diallo's team detected the behavior through the UEBA anomaly, not the DLP system. The evidence was collected with legal coordination, and the case was referred to internal investigations. The incident led to DLP enhancements including OCR (optical character recognition) scanning of image attachments, USB device restrictions, and enhanced UEBA monitoring for data access anomalies. **Lesson:** Insider threats are among the most challenging to detect because the adversary is a legitimate user with legitimate access. Behavioral analytics that detect deviations from normal patterns are more effective than rule-based controls that can be studied and circumvented. DLP must evolve beyond text-pattern matching.

### The PrintNightmare Detection Rule at Elastic
When the PrintNightmare vulnerability (CVE-2021-34527) was publicly disclosed, Diallo was on the Elastic Security detection engineering team. Within 2 hours of the disclosure, she had analyzed the exploitation mechanism (spooler service loading a malicious DLL), identified the forensic artifacts (spoolsv.exe creating DLL files in the system directory, unusual spoolsv.exe child processes), and written a detection rule in EQL (Event Query Language) that identified exploitation attempts. The rule was tested against a lab reproduction of the exploit, verified to produce zero false positives against a week of production telemetry from test environments, and deployed to Elastic's community detection rules repository within 6 hours of the original disclosure. The rule was automatically deployed to 10,000+ Elastic Security users through the detection rules update mechanism. Within the first 48 hours, the rule detected active exploitation attempts at 23 organizations, enabling containment before the attackers achieved their objectives. **Lesson:** Detection engineering for zero-day exploits requires speed, accuracy, and distribution capability. The value of a detection rule is proportional to how quickly it reaches defenders. Open-source detection rule repositories with automated distribution can protect thousands of organizations within hours of a new threat — faster than patching, which typically takes days to weeks.

## Red Flags & Anti-Patterns
- **"We have a SIEM, so we have a SOC"** — A SIEM is a tool, not a SOC. Without trained analysts, tuned detection rules, investigation procedures, and response capabilities, a SIEM is just an expensive log storage platform. The value of a SOC comes from the people and processes, not the technology.
- **"Our alert volume is 5,000 per day — we need more analysts"** — 5,000 alerts per day is not a staffing problem — it is a detection quality problem. More analysts will not fix a noisy detection system; they will just burn out faster. Before hiring, tune detection rules, automate triage with SOAR, implement risk-based alerting, and reduce the volume to a level that analysts can meaningfully investigate.
- **"We don't need threat hunting — our SIEM detects everything"** — SIEM detection rules detect known patterns. Novel techniques, living-off-the-land attacks, and sophisticated adversaries who study and evade common detection rules will not trigger SIEM alerts. Threat hunting is how SOCs discover the threats that automated detection misses.
- **"IOCs are our primary detection strategy"** — Indicators of Compromise (IP addresses, domain names, file hashes) are the lowest level of the Pyramid of Pain. Adversaries change IOCs trivially — a new domain costs $10, a new hash is one recompilation. Detection based primarily on IOCs requires constant updating and provides only temporary coverage. Behavioral detection (TTPs — Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) is durable because adversaries cannot easily change their behavior.
- **"The incident is resolved — the malware has been removed"** — Removing the malware addresses the symptom, not the root cause. How did the attacker gain initial access? Did they establish persistence mechanisms beyond the detected malware? Did they move laterally? Did they exfiltrate data? An incident is not resolved until root cause is determined, all attacker artifacts are removed, and prevention measures are in place.
- **"We run detection rules with default thresholds"** — Default thresholds are designed for generic environments. Every organization's telemetry patterns are unique. A detection rule that produces 2 false positives per day in one environment may produce 200 in another. Every rule must be tuned for the specific environment based on observed true and false positive rates.
